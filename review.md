        - The real-world experiment of the proposed approach is highly valuable, but the results are based on a single 580-second trip and are presented primarily via latency traces and a rolling average of video frame latency. Please explain precisely how the reported latencies were calculated, including which timestamps were recorded at the sender and receiver, how the ClockSynchronizer estimated the clock offset between the train and remote-control system, and what synchronization error remained after correction.
            - Thank you for pointing out the need for a more precise description of the latency measurement and clock synchronization procedure. We have clarified the latency calculation in the revised manuscript.

            - The latency measurements are based on timestamps from the physical clocks of the TrainClient and the RemoteControlClient. Since these systems do not share the same physical clock, a clock-offset estimation is performed before measuring the latency. The TrainClient initiates a clock-synchronization exchange by sending a message containing its local timestamp. When this message is received by the RemoteControlClient, it adds its own local  timestamp and returns the message to the TrainClient. Upon receiving the response the TrainClient calculates the Round Trip Time (RTT). Assuming that the propagation delay in both directions is approximately symmetric, the clock offset between the two systems is estimated from the remote timestamp and the midpoint of the local send and receive timestamps. The synchronization procedure is repeated 10 times, and the resulting offset estimates are averaged to reduce the influence of network. The RemoteControlClient performs the same procedure independently, with the roles of the two systems reversed. After synchronization, the TrainClient includes local timestamp with every transmitted video frame. When the Remote Control Client receives a frame, it records its local timestamp. The video-frame latency is then calculated with previously estimated clock offset. The command latency is measured analogously in the opposite direction. The RemoteControlClient timestamps each command before transmission, while the TrainClient timestamps its reception. The estimated clock offset is then applied to obtain the command latency.

            - To quantify the accuracy of the clock offset calculation, we conducted an experiment. The clock-offset calculation was performed 500 times, sent/received 5,000 clock-synchronization messages (10 messages per offset estimation). In this experiment, the same physical device was used to emulate both the TrainClient and the RemoteControlClient. Consequently, the expected clock offset was theoretically zero. Across the 500 offset estimations, the average absolute synchronization error was approximately 20 ms.

        - For the video frame latency measurements, please clarify whether Fig. 5 reports network transmission latency or the full capture-to-display latency.
            - <span style="color:green">Figure 5 reports the network transmission latency, measured from the timestamp assigned to a video frame at the Train Client to the timestamp when the corresponding frame is received by the Remote Control Client. It therefore does not represent the full capture-to-display latency. <b>We have clarified this distinction in the revised manuscript.</b></span>
        - This paper assumes approximately 50 ms for video processing and appears to use 150 ms as the network-latency budget under the 200 ms total requirement; this relationship should be stated explicitly.
            - <span style="color:green">We agree that the relationship between the assumed 50 ms processing time and the 200 ms total latency requirement could be made more explicit. Although the manuscript already stated the 200 ms requirement and the approximately 50 ms processing assumption, we have clarified that this leaves an effective 150 ms budget for network-induced latency. <b>The corresponding statement has been added to the revised manuscript.</b></span>
        - Please also provide summary statistics for the measured control-command and video latencies.
            - <span style="color:green"> Thank you for the suggestion. <b>We have added summary statistics for both the measured control-command and video latencies to the revised manuscript.</b></span>
        - Finally, the authors should clarify which WebTransport mechanisms are used for control commands and video, and distinguish the reliability and ordering guarantees of reliable streams from the behavior of unreliable datagrams. I believe these issues can be addressed before the final submission without changing the main contribution.
            - <span style="color:green"> We have clarified the WebTransport mechanisms used for the two data types in the revised manuscript. Control commands are transmitted over WebTransport reliable streams, which provide reliable and ordered delivery through QUIC. In contrast, video frames are transmitted using WebTransport unreliable datagrams, which do not provide delivery or ordering guarantees and do not retransmit lost packets. This allows video transmission to prioritize low latency over reliability, where losing an occasional frame is preferable to delaying subsequent frames through retransmission. <b>The corresponding clarification has been added to the revised manuscript.</b></span>
